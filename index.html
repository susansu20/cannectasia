Market Entry FAQ

Straight answers to the questions every board asks.

What is a market entry framework, and why does it matter for F&B?

A market entry framework is the structured sequence a brand follows to select, enter and scale in a new market: analysis, readiness, entry mode, plan, execution. In F&B it matters more than in most categories because supply chains, halal and regulatory requirements, real estate and taste preferences vary sharply between Asian markets. A generic framework misses exactly the variables that decide whether your first store trades.

Which Asian market should we enter first?

It depends on your format, price point, supply chain and capital. Singapore is a common proof-of-concept market for Western brands because of its transparency and talent, but it is not always the right first move. The diagnostic exists to answer this question for your brand specifically, with numbers.

Franchise, JV or corporate: which market entry mode is best?

None is best in the abstract. Franchising trades control for speed and local capital. Joint ventures balance control and local capability but demand careful deal structuring. Corporate entry maximises control and capital risk. We match the mode to each market's conditions and your appetite, and many successful regional plays mix modes across markets.

How long does market entry into Southeast Asia take?

From decision to first store, a well-run F&B market entry typically takes 9 to 18 months depending on the market, entry mode and how quickly the right partner and site are secured. The strategy phase itself usually runs 8 to 12 weeks.

Do you work with food products and FMCG brands, or only restaurants?

Both. Route-to-market design, distributor search and retail entry for packaged food, FMCG and manufacturing brands is one of our ten core services, run by specialists from those categories.

The Food and Beverage Industry in Malaysia: A 2026 Analysis for Entering Brands

By the CANnect Asia TeamUpdated July 20269 min read

Malaysia is one of the most frequently shortlisted markets in Southeast Asia for entering F&B brands, and one of the most frequently misread. The fundamentals are genuinely attractive: a population of around 34 million with a young, urban, digitally fluent consumer base, one of the region's strongest eating-out cultures, and a mall ecosystem that treats food and beverage as an anchor category rather than an afterthought. This analysis covers what has changed in the Malaysian food and beverage industry going into 2026, and what it means for restaurant chains, food products and FMCG brands weighing an entry.

Why Malaysia keeps making the shortlist

Three structural features explain Malaysia's pull. First, eating out is embedded in daily life across income levels, from 24-hour mamak culture to premium mall dining, which gives entering brands multiple viable price tiers rather than a single narrow segment. Second, the Klang Valley concentrates purchasing power, mall infrastructure and delivery density into a launch geography that can absorb a brand's first five to ten stores without leaving one metro area. Third, Malaysia often functions as a bridge market: more affordable to operate in than Singapore, more institutionally navigable than several emerging ASEAN peers, and culturally connected to both.

Malaysia rewards brands that treat it as a distinct market, and punishes brands that treat it as a discounted Singapore.

The market in 2026: what has actually changed

The post-pandemic recovery phase is over; what remains is a structurally different market. Delivery has settled into a permanent share of F&B revenue rather than a temporary spike, which changes format economics: kitchens are sized differently, seating ratios have shifted and rents are being renegotiated around blended dine-in and delivery revenue. Consumers have become simultaneously more value-conscious in everyday spending and more willing to pay for genuinely differentiated experiences, squeezing the undifferentiated middle. Tourism inflows, supported by the national Visit Malaysia 2026 campaign, are lifting footfall in key retail corridors, which matters most for flagship and airport-adjacent formats.

Halal is a system, not a certificate

For most entering brands, halal strategy is the single most consequential early decision, and the most commonly underestimated. Certification through JAKIM, Malaysia's federal Islamic authority, is not a one-time stamp: it reaches into supply chain sourcing, kitchen segregation, logistics and ongoing audit. Brands have three broad positions available: full certification, halal-compliant sourcing without certification, or a deliberate non-halal positioning serving specific segments. All three can work commercially. What does not work is deciding late, because the choice shapes site selection, supplier contracts and even franchise partner profiles from day one.

Where brands actually trade: malls, shoplots and delivery

  • Malls remain the primary discovery channel for international brands. Relationships with major mall operators matter, and rental structures often include gross-turnover components that entering brands must model correctly.
  • Shoplots and standalone sites offer lower rents and stronger neighbourhood loyalty, suited to brands with established local awareness or strong delivery economics.
  • Delivery-led and hybrid formats allow market testing at lower capital risk, though they build revenue faster than they build brand.

Franchising in Malaysia: regulated, and that is good news

Malaysia is one of the few markets in the region with a dedicated franchise statute. The Franchise Act 1998 requires franchisors to register before offering franchises in Malaysia, mandates disclosure and sets minimum terms. For disciplined brands this is an advantage: the regulatory framework filters out casual operators and gives well-prepared franchisors a credibility marker. For unprepared brands it is a timeline surprise, because registration must be sequenced into the entry plan rather than discovered during partner negotiations. Food franchising specifically remains one of Malaysia's most active franchise categories, and partner quality varies as widely as anywhere in the region, which is precisely why partner vetting matters more than partner finding.

Entry modes that work in Malaysia

There is no single correct market entry mode for Malaysia, but there are patterns. Master franchising suits proven multi-unit brands with strong systems and a partner-quality bar they refuse to lower. Joint ventures suit brands that need local capability, particularly around halal supply chains and mall relationships, but want to retain meaningful control. Direct corporate entry suits well-capitalised brands using Malaysia as a regional operating base. For food products and FMCG, distribution partnerships remain the default route, with channel fit, modern trade versus general trade versus foodservice, deciding more than distributor size.

The five most common failure points

  • Treating Malaysia as one market instead of several: the Klang Valley, Penang, Johor and East Malaysia behave differently in taste, price tolerance and real estate.
  • Deciding halal positioning after signing the first lease.
  • Pricing transplanted from Singapore without rebuilding the unit economics.
  • Selecting the first willing franchise partner rather than the right one.
  • Under-modelling gross-turnover rent components in mall agreements.
The brands that win in Malaysia decide their halal position, their price architecture and their partner profile before the first flight, not after the first quarter.

Frequently asked questions

Is Malaysia a good first market for entering Southeast Asia?

Often, yes, particularly for brands whose economics need a lower cost base than Singapore but whose operations need more infrastructure than emerging ASEAN markets offer. Whether it should be first for your brand depends on your format, halal position, capital and supply chain. That is a diagnostic question, not a general one.

Do foreign F&B brands need halal certification in Malaysia?

No, certification is not legally mandatory for restaurants, but the commercial reality is that halal status determines your addressable market, your mall options and your partner pool. The decision should be made deliberately and early, whichever way it goes.

What does the Franchise Act 1998 mean for entering franchisors?

Franchisors must register with the Malaysian franchise registrar before offering franchises, provide mandated disclosure and comply with minimum contract terms. Sequenced properly, registration is a manageable workstream; discovered late, it can stall a signed partner for months.

How long does it take to open a restaurant in Malaysia?

For a foreign brand entering via a local partner, expect 6 to 12 months from decision to first trading day, depending on entry mode, franchise registration where applicable, site availability and fit-out. Licensing at the local council level is procedural but must be built into the critical path.
